## Read this before quoting any number

- **Studio play mode is loopback.** The replication path is real, the network is not. These
  results support relative comparison between libraries and nothing else. They are not
  bandwidth or latency figures.
- **Bytes per packet is exact**, counted by `WireTap` off each library's own remote
  (`buffer.len`), not derived from `Stats`. `raw` has no buffer to measure, so its byte column
  is empty rather than zero.
- ~~**netweave is measured behind a stand-in transport.**~~ **True of the two M1 runs only.** They
  measure the codec plus a stand-in written to satisfy `docs/WIRE-FORMAT.md` §1, because batching,
  budgets, audience evaluation and coalescing were a milestone away. The M2 run below has no
  stand-in: the netweave column is `src/transport/`. Read the M1 tables with that asterisk and the
  M2 section without it.
- **The Studio-only `ctx` guard was on for the M1 runs, and off for the M2 run.** It allocates
  two tables per packet and
  exists only in Studio. The M1 decode-allocation figures are therefore high by whatever it costs;
  the M2 adapter turns it off explicitly, so the M2 figures are not.
- **Allocation figures are order-of-magnitude only, and the run-to-run variance is larger than
  the differences between libraries.** Zap's `ArrayHeavy` decode measured 1474.6 B on 2026-09-03
  and 65.5 B here — the same library, the same schema, a 22x swing. Roblox permits
  `collectgarbage("count")` but not stopping the collector, so windows it disturbed are discarded
  and the median of the rest is reported. Treat a 2x difference in these columns as noise.
- **Every allocation median is printed with the range of its surviving windows and the count of
  them. Read those before quoting the median** — a cell with four windows spanning a factor of
  sixteen is not a number, and the M3 section below shows that describes every `ArrayHeavy` encode
  cell in the matrix. See `bench/README.md`, "Reading the allocation numbers", for how this was
  established and what it cost to learn.
- Framerate is the p50 of one-second samples. Studio runs uncapped, so absolute values are much
  higher than a shipped game would see; only the ratios matter.

## Bytes on the wire

Includes the 1-byte packet id every library prefixes.

| Schema | netweave | Blink | Zap | ByteNet | raw |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `ArrayHeavy` (100 x 6 x u8) | **601** | **601** | **601** | 603 | not measurable |
| `FlagIdiomatic` | 8 | 10 | **7** | 20 | not measurable |
| `FlagNaive` | 8 | 20 | **7** | 20 | not measurable |

netweave's figures carry a fractional part in the raw document — 601.005 and 8.005 — because the
wire tap counts whole batches and `WIRE-FORMAT.md` §1 puts one version byte on each. At 200
packets a batch that byte is 0.005 per packet. It is the only per-batch cost in the format.

**The array payload is a three-way tie at the theoretical floor.** 600 bytes of data and one byte
of channel id, with nothing else on the wire. A static schema needs no length prefix, so G5's
framing guarantee is free here — exactly the prediction in `WIRE-FORMAT.md` §2, now measured.

**On flags netweave pays one byte more than Zap, and that byte is the guarantee.** 8 B against
7 B is the length prefix that lets a failed decode find the next packet boundary. Blink and Zap
have no length field, so one malformed packet costs the rest of the batch (`RESEARCH §3.8-R`).
`bench/envelope.luau` demonstrates the difference directly: a refused packet is skipped and the
one behind it decodes. That is what the byte buys, and 14% on a 7-byte payload is what it costs.

**The two flag columns are the automatic-packing claim, measured.** netweave and Zap pay the same
8 B and 7 B in both, because both pack flags, enum tags and presence bits without being asked.
Blink pays 10 B or 20 B *for the same schema*, depending on whether its author knew about the
`set` type. ByteNet has neither `set` nor an enum type and pays 20 B either way — that is what a
ByteNet author can express, not a handicap imposed here.

This is PLAN-M1 acceptance criterion 4, and it is met: equal to Blink and Zap on `ArrayHeavy`,
within one byte of Zap on `FlagIdiomatic`.

**netweave misses acceptance criterion 5 on the array family.** The criterion is 1.3x of the best
library per schema family; netweave is at 1.64x of Blink and 1.36x of Zap on `ArrayHeavy`. On both
flag families it passes, and on `FlagNaive` it is the fastest mode measured.

The direction of the miss is specific, and the other two directions say where it is not:

| `ArrayHeavy`, p50 | netweave | Blink | Zap | ByteNet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Up — the client **encodes** | 85 | **139** | 116 | 120 |
| Down — the client **decodes** | **80** | 71 | 70 | 55 |
| FireAll — the client decodes | 77 | **79** | 69 | 58 |

**netweave decodes the array payload faster than any of them and encodes it slower than two of
them.** Decode is where `table.clone(TEMPLATE)` pays off (`RESEARCH §3.8-S`), and this is the
first measurement of that as a framerate rather than as an allocation count.

### The codegen gap, per schema family

PLAN-M1 phase 6 asks for this as a percentage. netweave against the better of the two code
generators, Blink and Zap, in the same cell of the same run:

| | Encode (Up) | Decode (Down) |
|---|---|---|
| `ArrayHeavy` | **0.61x — 39% behind** | 1.13x — 13% ahead |
| `FlagIdiomatic` | 1.00x | 0.96x |
| `FlagNaive` | 1.03x | 0.88x |

**Only one of those six numbers is outside the noise.** The same libraries measured across the
two runs a day apart move by up to 10% on framerate with no code change at all — Blink's
`FlagNaive` encode went 211 to 232, Zap's `ArrayHeavy` encode 125 to 116. So the honest reading
of this table is: netweave is 39% behind on array encode, indistinguishable everywhere else, and
the 13% decode lead is real but only just.

One cell, one direction. That is the whole measured gap, and it has an identified cause.

Two things are wrong on the encode side, and the second is much larger than the first.

**The adapter allocated two tables per send.** The stand-in transport called `Buffer.save()` twice
to swap destination buffers, whatever the payload size — the flat 609.3 B encode figure below is
that, and at 200 packets a frame it is 400 tables a frame of waste. Fixed after this run.

**`Serdes` never calls `Buffer.allocate`.** Every scalar goes through `Buffer.writeU8` and friends,
and each of those allocates its own one to eight bytes: bounds check, growth check, cursor update,
per field. One `ArrayHeavy` packet is 100 structs of six `u8`, so **600 allocations per packet and
120,000 per frame**; a flag packet is six, so 1,200 per frame. Blink and Zap emit one allocation
for a whole block and then write at `offset + k`.

That is `PLAN-M1` D-2's first layout pass — "fixed-size prefix calculation so the top level
allocates once" — computed and then not used. `Ir.lower` produces `fixedSize`, `Serdes.build`
copies it onto the `Codec`, and nothing reads it. **It explains the shape of the data in a way the
adapter defect cannot:** the gap appears only where the payload has hundreds of fields, and
vanishes where it has six.

**The M0 finding that raw beats every library on small payloads did not reproduce.** On
`FlagIdiomatic` raw is 233 against ByteNet's 234 and netweave's 218 — a tie rather than a win, and
on `FlagNaive` raw is last at 208. The 2026-09-03 run had raw ahead in both. The crossover exists
but it is closer than one run suggested.

## Re-measurement after the encode fix

`bench/runs/2026-09-04-recheck.json` — netweave, Blink and Zap only, same load, same windows,
taken after the adapter stopped allocating two tables per send. Nine cells, ten minutes.

| `ArrayHeavy`, Up (encode) | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| netweave | 85 | **85** |
| Blink | 139 | 144 |
| Zap | 116 | 120 |

**The fix changed nothing, and that is the answer.** Blink and Zap both moved up by 4 FPS — the
machine was slightly quicker this time — while netweave sat on exactly 85. The gap widened from
1.64x to 1.69x for that reason alone.

So the two hypotheses separate cleanly:

- **The adapter's two tables per send were not the cause.** They are gone, and on the flag schemas
  where they *were* the whole measurement the encode figure fell from 609.3 B to **81.92 B, Zap's
  number to the decimal**. The fix worked; it just was not what mattered on arrays.
- **`Serdes` never calling `Buffer.allocate` stands as the cause.** 600 allocations for one
  `ArrayHeavy` packet against six for a flag packet is the only explanation offered so far that
  predicts a gap on one schema family and none on the other, and the re-measurement did not
  falsify it.

The `ArrayHeavy` encode-allocation cell reads 3932.2 B afterwards against 609.3 before, which
looks like a regression and is not readable: 4 of 25 windows survived the collector against 24
before, and the figure is bit-identical to Zap's in the same run because both landed in the same
quantisation bucket. Nothing should be concluded from that cell.

### The noise floor, measured directly

netweave declares `FlagIdiomatic` and `FlagNaive` with **the same schema** — automatic packing
leaves nothing for an author to get wrong, which is the point of the pair. So its two flag cells
run identical code on identical data in the same run, and any difference between them is the
harness:

| netweave, Up | `FlagIdiomatic` | `FlagNaive` |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-09-04 | 218 | 238 |
| recheck | 237 | 208 |

**A 14% spread between two cells that cannot differ.** That is a better estimate of this
harness's precision than the between-run comparison, and it retires every flag-column difference
in both runs: netweave, Blink and Zap are indistinguishable there. Only the `ArrayHeavy` encode
gap, at 69%, is outside it — by a factor of five.

## M2: the stand-in replaced by the real transport

`bench/runs/2026-09-04-m2.json`. The same place, the same load, the same wire tap — but the
netweave column now runs `src/transport/`: `Batch` framing, `Outbound` fan-out with per-recipient
parked buffers, `Inbound` read-then-dispatch, `Budget` enforcement, audience evaluation and intent
coalescing. The adapter implements no transport at all any more; it declares nine channels and
calls the views.

The test suite ran in the same Play, ahead of the matrix: **8 runtime modules, 8 passed.**

| netweave, Up | stand-in (recheck) | real transport | best rival, this run |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ArrayHeavy` bytes | 601 | **601** | 601 (Blink, Zap) |
| `ArrayHeavy` framerate p50 | 85 | **86** | 132 (Blink) |
| `ArrayHeavy` encode B/call | 3932.2 | **3932.2** | 2836.5 (ByteNet) |
| `ArrayHeavy` decode B/packet | 1184.8 | **9309.2** | 258.0 (Zap) |
| `FlagIdiomatic` bytes | 8 | **8** | 7 (Zap) |
| `FlagIdiomatic` framerate p50 | 237 | **216** | 234 (Blink) |
| `Flag*` encode B/call | 81.9 | **81.9** | 81.9 (Zap) |
| `Flag*` decode B/packet | 573.4 | **462.8** | 573.4 (Zap) |

Four things follow, and one of them is bad.

**The wire did not move.** 601 B and 8 B, byte for byte, with the real envelope in place of the
stand-in that existed only to satisfy `WIRE-FORMAT.md` §1. The format survived contact with its own
implementation — the one thing a stand-in cannot establish about a format.

**Delivery is exact.** netweave is the only column where `sent == received` in every schema:
173,200, 437,000 and 472,400 with no shortfall. Every other library loses the same 200 packets, a
frame's worth in flight when the counters are read; netweave's `PostSimulation` flush drains before
the sample closes. 45 of 45 cells passed payload validation.

**Flag decode allocation is now the best of the field** — 462.8 B against Zap's 573.4 B, ByteNet's
593.9 B and Blink's 880.6 B — and it improved by *leaving* the stand-in, without the codec changing.

**And `ArrayHeavy` decode allocation went 1184.8 B to 9309.2 B, 7.9x, the worst cell in the run.**
The cause is a decision rather than a defect. `Inbound` reads a whole batch to completion before
dispatching any of it, because a yielding handler plus a global decode cursor attributes the *next*
batch's bytes to *this* batch's sender (`PLAN-M2` phase 3, with a regression test that fails against
the old code). At 200 packets per frame that leaves 200 decoded `ArrayHeavy` values live at once,
roughly 1.8 MB, where the stand-in held one and let the collector take it between packets.

Framerate is unchanged across that change — 85 to 86 — so the cost is peak memory, not CPU. It is
bounded by one batch. But the size of that batch is chosen by an untrusted client, which makes it a
resource limit rather than a performance note. **It is M3's, and it is written down there.**

**The `ArrayHeavy` framerate gap is the codec's, and this run isolates it.** 85 with the stand-in,
86 with batching, budget accounting, audience evaluation and per-packet isolation added — M2 cost
nothing measurable. Whatever puts netweave at 86 against Blink's 132 was already there in M1, on the
array decode path, and no transport work will move it.

Re-judged against `PLAN-M1`'s acceptance criteria on this run: **criterion 5 is still missed** —
86 against Blink's 132 is 1.53x, past the 1.3x bar, on `ArrayHeavy` only — and **criterion 6 has
gone from met to missed on `ArrayHeavy`**, 9309.2 B against ByteNet's 2273.3 B, while staying met on
both flag families at 462.8 B against 593.9 B. Both are the same read-then-dispatch trade, and both
were bought deliberately with a correctness fix.

Encode survives four to eight windows out of twenty-five and is not a measurement at all. Decode

**The reason is the size of what is being measured.** netweave's `ArrayHeavy` decode is ~10 KB per

loses**, and the cells it cannot resolve are the ones a reader would quote against it.

That is a limit of the instrument and it is not fixed by running it again. What would fix it is a
per-frame window rather than a per-packet one — measured under lune in phase 9 at 400 usable windows
out of 400 with a spread of zero — and porting that to Studio is M4's, not this run's.

## M4 phase 0: the instrument, rebuilt on the send side

`bench/runs/2026-09-05-m4p0-a.json` and `-b.json`, the same tree run twice. **This section is about
the harness, not about any library** — M3 closed with the allocation column unable to tell a change
from the collector, and M4 cannot measure delta compression on an instrument like that.

### What was wrong

The encode probe called `fire` five thousand times back to back and **never let a frame end**. For a
batching library that means nothing is ever flushed: the payloads pile into one outgoing buffer that
grows by doubling, so most calls allocate nothing and a handful allocate a block the size of
everything before them. The median of twenty-five windows over that is a lottery on where the
doublings landed.

The window is one frame now, ending in a yield so every library's own scheduler flushes inside it.

| `ArrayHeavy` encode | M3 instrument | M4 phase 0, run A | run B |
|---|---|---|---|
| netweave | 3932.2 <sub>[1311 .. 20972]</sub>, n=4 | **1909.8** <sub>[1884 .. 1925]</sub>, n=326 | **1920.0** |
| blink | 4039.7 <sub>[2662 .. 6057]</sub>, n=5 | **2718.7** <sub>[2708 .. 2734]</sub>, n=282 | **2729.0** |
| zap | 3932.2, n=4 | **1920.0** <sub>[1823 .. 1925]</sub>, n=320 | **1920.0** |
| bytenet | 3722.2, n=7 | **614.4** <sub>[589 .. 625]</sub>, n=373 | **604.2** |
| raw | no usable window, ever | **10.2** <sub>[5 .. 31]</sub>, n=302 | **10.2** |

Surviving windows went from four-to-eight of twenty-five to **276-385 of 400**, the `ArrayHeavy`
range from sixteen times to **0.3%**, and the two runs agree to **1.7% or better on every cell**.

**The old numbers were not merely imprecise, they were biased high by a factor of two to six** —
which is what an unflushed doubling buffer does. Two independent checks say the new ones are right:

- netweave's 1909.8 B agrees with the phase 9 lune probe's **1908.4 B to 0.07%**, from a different
  runtime through a different code path;
- `raw`, which does no serialisation at all, lands on **10.24 B** — exactly the idle control group's
  figure, because all that is left in its window is the engine.

The ranking changed with the precision. The M3 column read blink 4040 / netweave 3932 / zap 3932 /
bytenet 3722 — four libraries within 8%, which is to say a tie, which is to say noise. What is
actually there is **bytenet at a third of the field**, netweave and zap together, and blink 42%
behind them.

### The idle control group

`idle` in the run document is a frame that sends nothing, measured in the same units and window
count: **10.24 B per packet-equivalent, 400/400 windows**, in both runs. A frame window contains a
real engine frame, so Studio's own allocation is inside every encode cell and this is how much of
it. Negligible against `ArrayHeavy`; about a quarter of a flag cell, which is why it is printed
above the table rather than left implicit.

### What phase 0 tried on the receive side and reverted

The same frame window was applied to decode and then taken back out, because the two costs have
opposite shapes. An outgoing buffer is still there at the frame boundary; a batch of decoded values
is not — `inbound.receive` decodes, dispatches and returns its pending list to the pool, so a
`PostSimulation` sample arrives *after* the peak it is trying to measure.

Settled by measurement rather than argument. One decoded `ArrayHeavy` value is **32,097 bytes** under
lune, which agrees with phase 9's independent probe to 0.15%:

| `ArrayHeavy` decode, per packet | reported | against 32,097 B |
|---|---|---|
| frame boundary | 604 | out by 53x |
| packet-aligned, inside the callback | ~10,200 | out by 3.2x |

So the decode window stayed where it was. **Both are lower bounds**, because every window the
collector visited is discarded and the survivors are the ones where it freed the least. Read the
Studio decode column as "at least this much".

### Where that leaves acceptance 9

`PLAN-M4` criterion 9 asks for two runs of the unchanged tree agreeing within 10% on every cell.

- **Encode `ArrayHeavy`: met**, every library within 1.7%.
- **Encode flags: at the floor rather than met.** The cells move by one or two steps of 5.12 B,
  which is 10-33% of a number that is only four to eight steps above zero. `collectgarbage("count")`
  reports kilobytes, so a frame window resolves to ±1024 B — ±5.12 B per packet at this load — and
  no arrangement of windows improves on that.
- **Decode flags: met, and exactly.** 462.848 / 880.64 / 593.92 / 573.44 / 561.152 in both runs, for
  all five modes, to the decimal.
- **Decode `ArrayHeavy`: not met.** netweave −13%, blink −17%, zap −4%, bytenet +7.5%. Better than
  the M3 instrument, where the same two cells moved **76% and 146%**, and still outside the bar.

The criterion stays open on one column, and the reason is now understood rather than merely
observed: a window large enough to hold a `ArrayHeavy` batch is a window the collector almost always
visits, and Roblox exposes enough to detect that and not enough to correct for it.

netweave still allocates 6.6x less than Blink and 6.4x less than Zap despite the waste; on the
flag schemas the waste is the entire figure and netweave is 7x worse than Zap.

**Decode on the array payload: 389.1 B against ByteNet's 520.2 B.** PLAN-M1 acceptance criterion 6
asked for at or below ByteNet's, and that is met — but the criterion was written when ByteNet's
389 B was the best number anyone had. Zap's 65.5 B here resets the bar by a factor of six, and it
did not reproduce Zap's own 1474.6 B from the previous run, so the honest reading is that this
column needs a better instrument before it decides anything.

netweave's decode figure also includes the Studio-only `ctx` guard, which was on for this run.

## What this fixes about the plan

- **PLAN-M1 D-3 is confirmed.** "There is no byte win available against Zap on packing" — measured
  at 7 B against 8 B, the difference being the length prefix rather than the packing. netweave
  reaches parity with Zap on the array payload and beats Blink on both flag columns, which is what
  D-3 predicted after it corrected itself.
- **`WIRE-FORMAT.md` §2's cost estimate is confirmed exactly.** 0% on `ArrayHeavy`, one byte on
  `FlagIdiomatic`. The `derived` framing in §7 that would recover it is still deferred.
- **`RESEARCH §3.7-D` needs revising, not deleting.** It framed allocation coalescing as the
  advantage a code generator has over a runtime schema. This run shows the split is by *direction*:
  the generators lead on encode and netweave leads on decode, on the same payload in the same run.
  §3.7-D should be narrowed to encode.
- **PLAN-M0's "raw beats libraries on small payloads" is weakened.** It reproduced as a tie, not a
  win. One run is not enough to state it as a rule.
- **The harness cannot resolve differences under about 15%.** Measured directly, not estimated:
  netweave's two flag cells run the same schema through the same code and differ by 14% in the same
  run. Every future claim from this harness has to clear that bar, and most of the flag-column
  differences in both runs do not.
